# CS-250: Software Development Lifecycle

## Sprint Review and Retrospective

📄 [View Sprint Review and Retrospective (PDF)](Sprint_Review_and_Retrospective.pdf)

This repository contains my Sprint Review and Retrospective from CS-250: Software Development Lifecycle. The project was part of a simulated Agile environment in which I took on all four Scrum roles—Scrum Master, Product Owner, Developer, and Tester—while developing the SNHU Travel application. This final reflection highlights my experience across roles and demonstrates how Agile principles shaped each phase of the project.

## Reflection

Working through this simulation helped me understand how user needs evolve and how Agile frameworks allow teams to continuously align with those needs. At the start of the sprint, the Product Owner facilitated a focus group that revealed valuable insights—many of which weren’t captured in the initial scope. Through that session, we created user stories that reflected real customer expectations, such as filtering destinations by travel history, budget, and trip type. These stories grounded our work and provided clear acceptance criteria that guided development and testing.

User stories played a central role in interpreting and implementing user needs. They acted as both a planning and communication tool, helping the team break down features into manageable tasks. Agile events like Sprint Planning, Daily Standups, and Backlog Refinement allowed us to estimate effort, surface blockers, and realign priorities as needed. When the project pivoted mid-sprint from general travel to wellness-focused content, the team was able to respond quickly without derailing progress. The existing structure was adapted with minimal rework, demonstrating how Agile supports flexibility through steady, iterative delivery.

Even in a simulated environment, it was clear that strong communication makes or breaks a sprint. I had opportunities to write cross-role messages that clarified expectations, aligned goals, and prevented unnecessary rework. These realistic interactions made the process feel collaborative and reinforced the importance of surfacing blockers early. As a Developer and Tester, I saw firsthand how early feedback reduced confusion, especially when UI behavior or story assumptions didn’t match.

Through each role, I learned that being a good team member in software development isn’t just about completing tasks—it’s about listening, asking the right questions, and helping the team move forward together. The Scrum framework provided the rhythm and accountability needed to stay focused, while tools like a Trello-style board helped visualize progress and support backlog discussions. 

This experience reaffirmed that I want to carry Agile into future projects. It’s not just a methodology—it’s a mindset of adaptability, collaboration, and continuous improvement. 

Whether I’m developing software or leading a team, I plan to build with the user in mind, deliver value iteratively, and create space for feedback at every stage.
